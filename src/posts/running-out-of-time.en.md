Since I was a kid I have felt like I don't have time. Maybe because I was the youngest in my family, between my brother and my cousins. I always wanted to be doing what they were doing. That's how I learned to walk. I wanted to play with my older brother, but my concept of playing at the time was picking up everything he was doing, so to gain time he would put me at the bottom of the hallway and it took me a while to crawl to his room. One of those times, my brain probably worked out that I would be faster walking. That's how I took my first steps, and my mother thought he was making it up when he screamed that I was on my feet.

Over time it became a family joke that I took my first steps in the direction of my brother. It's no secret to anyone who knows us that he's my role model. He always was. Everything he did I tried to do too, and that's probably why I became a data journalist. We had the same genes and the same upbringing, and we're the most similar people you could know. His girlfriend and my boyfriend are always making that joke.

We were happy, but we were taught that being good at what you do is the bare minimum and that work pays off. That's what happens when your grandparents grew up in poverty and tried everything not to be poor, and were emigrants, and their children got mocked at school for being the children of Portuguese parents. My grandparents' biggest accomplishment was owning a house and putting one of their children through nursing school.

My parents knew where they had been raised and tried to make their children's lives a lot better. They wanted to give my brother and me a better life. Full of education, newspapers and a lot of extracurricular activities.

I had it a lot easier than my brother, that's the good part of being the youngest. We were both very good students. Everyone thought he would be a doctor or an engineer. For me, engineer was the answer. Plot twist: we both decided to be journalists. Yeah. All those years of good grades and a lot of mathematics and you end up in a job that doesn't pay all that well, and then you have the nerve to go and mix mathematics and programming back into it.

The result, twenty-odd years later, is a list of ideas that grows faster than my capacity to touch any of them. And they're nearly all work ideas, which says something about me already. I don't daydream about trips, I daydream about projects. Some days I open my laptop wanting to start three things at once. Not as a turn of phrase, actually three: one that should have been finished ages ago, one that showed up in my head in the shower, and one that only exists because I saw somebody else's nice chart and thought it could be a good starting point for something in Portugal, with a bit of envy mixed in for not having had the idea first. Each one, if I'm honest, needs two or three weeks of real work.

And even the ideas that aren't work ideas ask for exactly the same things: time, a working head and study. I'd enrol in a PhD tomorrow if I could. I'd read every book in the world. I'd like to finally learn how not to kill the plants at home, and I suspect that would end with me reading three books about watering before I picked up a watering can. Maybe this doesn't count as resting. In my head it counts.

And I'm one person. That's the part I find hardest to admit. It isn't a shortage of time in the abstract, it's that there's one of me, with one pair of hands, and the ideas don't queue up, they all arrive at once and sit there looking at me.

Then there's the other urge, which turns up at exactly the same moment and doesn't ask permission. Close everything. Go for a walk. Read something useless, something that's pure entertainment and nothing else. Go to ballet and be bad at it for an hour with no consequences. Spend a whole Saturday producing absolutely nothing and not feel like I should be somewhere else.

Both urges live in the same head at the same time, and it's ridiculous.

2026 made all of it louder. I won't go into details, but it has been the hardest year of my life. The kind of hard that changes your brain chemistry, where you ask whether you and the people you love most will ever be at peace, or happy, again. The kind of thing that makes you feel you control nothing and that your time here is very short.

That isn't a metaphor, it's what actually happens: you start looking at the calendar differently, and there's a new urgency underneath everything, even underneath the small stuff.

And the urgency cuts both ways, which is the part nobody explains. Some days it makes me want to get everything done, now, while I still can. Other days it makes me look at the list of ideas and find it a bit obscene. I'm going to die and someone is going to look at my laptop and find folders of unfinished projects.

Except the obvious answer, drop it all and go live, doesn't work for me either. Because I like this. I really do. The ideas don't show up out of obligation, they show up because I find them funny, because I want to see the thing finished, because there's a stupid pleasure in watching something that only existed in my head start working in a browser. If I dropped everything and went off to live, what I'd want to do with the free time would very probably be a project.

So I keep picking one thing at a time, badly, and letting the rest rot on the list. And I keep feeling guilty about both things at once, for not doing more and for not stopping. I don't have a method. I'm not going to say I learned to say no, because I didn't.

This year I watched one of the most important people in my life relearn things he already knew how to do at two years old. I still don't know what to do with that.
